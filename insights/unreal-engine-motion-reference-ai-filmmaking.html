The Problem With Prompting Motion

Video models got very good at how things look. They are still bad at taking direction. Ask for "the camera arcs left as she turns at the seven second mark" and you get a camera that drifts somewhere, a turn that happens whenever, and a different layout on every try.

That is fine for a mood clip. It breaks down the moment you need a real scene: a fight where each hit lands on a beat, a dialogue where the cut lands on the right face, a car that drifts through the exact corner you planned. Every reroll costs money and time, and you are still guessing.

The fix is not a better prompt. It is to stop describing motion in words and start showing it.

Block First, Generate Second

Filmmakers already solved this problem decades ago. It is called previz. You block the scene with stand-ins, lock the camera and the timing, and only then spend money on the real shot.

The same idea now works with generative video. You build a rough 3D version of the shot: grey boxes for the set, simple mannequins for the actors, a real animated camera. You render that as a plain video and give it to a video-to-video model as the motion reference. The model keeps the camera path, the blocking and the timing frame for frame. Reference images tell it what the people and the world should look like.

The split that makes it work: the 3D pass owns everything that moves. The model owns everything that is seen. Camera, timing, layout and performance come from the render. Faces, costumes, materials and light come from the references and the prompt.

Two things fall out of this for free. First, iteration gets cheap: if a cut is wrong, you fix it in the 3D scene in a minute instead of rolling the model ten more times. Second, the blocking becomes reusable. Keep the same 30 seconds of camera work and render it as a night chase, an anime short or a toy commercial, just by swapping the references.

Why Unreal Engine Makes a Great Motion Engine

Any 3D tool can produce a blocking pass. Unreal Engine has a few advantages that matter a lot once you do this every day.

It plays back in real time

You scrub the whole shot at full speed, with the final lens and the final timing, and see it the way the model will. No waiting for a viewport preview to catch up. When the director says "earlier", you move a key and watch it again straight away.

Sequencer is a real camera department

Cine Camera actors with real lenses and focus, camera rails and cranes, shot tracks with cuts that land on exact frames, and takes you can compare side by side. That is the language of the people who will judge the final film.

Real performances, not stiff mannequins

The most common complaint about this workflow is that the generated actors move like the proxies: stiff and robotic. The model copies the motion it is given. Unreal lets you give it better motion. Record an actor with a mocap suit or a phone, stream it through Live Link onto the mannequin, or clean it up with Control Rig. The proxy stays a grey shape, but it moves with human weight and timing, and so does the result.

Physics, crowds and vehicles

Chaos physics gives you crashes, falling debris and cloth with real momentum. Mass gives you crowds. Vehicle templates give you drifts with believable weight transfer. These are exactly the moments where a pure prompt falls apart, and they cost nothing to simulate first.

Agents can build the blocking for you

You do not need to be a 3D artist anymore. With an AI agent connected to the editor, you describe the scene in plain words ("a corridor, six people around a table, an orbit that slows on each face") and the agent places the proxies, builds the camera and keys the timing. The Workflow, Step by Step

1. Block the set

Grey boxes for walls, props and vehicles. Match the real proportions of the location, because the model will keep them. A black and white checkerboard on floors and large surfaces helps the model read speed and direction during fast camera moves.

2. Make the proxies readable

Give every character one solid colour and keep it for the whole project: blue is the woman, orange is the man, and so on. Then you can say "the red figure" in the prompt and the model knows exactly who you mean. Mark which way each proxy faces, for example a small cone on the head like a nose, so the model never turns a character around by mistake.

3. Animate with real motion

Use mocap or hand-keyed Control Rig for anything with weight: fights, falls, dialogue gestures. Keep the counts honest. If six people are in the scene, six proxies are in the scene, standing where they will stand.

4. Shoot it in Sequencer

Pick the lens on purpose. Put moves on rails so they are smooth. If you want handheld, add slow body sway, not random jitter. Make every cut land on a frame, with camera, target and focal length changing exactly on the cut.

5. Record the reference pass

Render the shot as a plain, flat-lit video: 1920x1080 at 24 frames per second is a good default, frame-aligned and the same length as the clip you want back. Keep it unlit or clay-shaded so the model takes motion from it and nothing else. Some video models also accept extra guide passes such as depth, normals or line art, which Unreal can output from the same shot.

6. Write the prompt like a call sheet

The best prompts for this workflow read like instructions to a crew, not like poetry. Tell the model what each input is for and what it must not take from it:

  • The video is the only authority for camera, timing, layout and who stands where. Take no colours, materials or lighting from it.
  • Each reference image defines one character's face and costume, or one location's look. Take no pose, camera or background from it.
  • Timed beats say what happens when: "at 12 seconds the first attacker falls".
  • Hard rules stop the usual failures: exact head counts, nobody entering from off screen, the camera following the reference one to one.

7. Iterate on the blocking, not the prompt

When a shot is wrong, the fix usually belongs in the 3D scene: move the cut, slow the move, shift an actor. Re-record and regenerate. When the shot is right, keep the blocking and swap the look for a new version.

Recording the Reference Pass

For a locked shot from Sequencer, Movie Render Queue is the standard choice and works well. It renders offline, frame by frame.

The interesting cases are the live ones. A director scouting the set in VR, a camera operator with a virtual camera on an iPad, an actor driving a character in real time, a vehicle driven by hand around the track. Those performances happen once, in real time, and you want them on video as they happen.

That is where Runtime Video Recorder fits. It records straight from the running engine with hardware encoding, on desktop, mobile and Meta Quest, and can lock the output to an exact frame rate so a 24 fps reference stays frame-aligned. You can record several cameras at once (see multi-camera recording) and the result is a plain MP4 you can hand to the video model straight away.

Limits Worth Knowing

  • The model copies what you give it. Stiff proxy animation becomes stiff acting. Put the effort into the motion pass.
  • Long clips drift. Identity and counts get shaky over very long takes. Plan cuts in the blocking and generate shot by shot when you need control.
  • Faces need strong references. One clean character sheet per actor beats a dozen loose photos.
  • Use motion you own. Capture your own performers, and get consent before you use anyone's likeness or voice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a motion reference in AI video?

A motion reference is a video you give a video-to-video model to control movement. The model keeps the camera path, the timing and the positions of the characters from that video, and uses reference images and the prompt to decide what everything looks like.

Why use Unreal Engine instead of prompting the camera move?

Text can only describe motion, and models interpret it differently on every try. A rendered blocking pass shows the exact move, timing and layout, so the result matches your plan and you stop paying for rerolls. Unreal adds real-time playback, a full camera toolset in Sequencer, physics and live mocap.

Do I need to be a 3D artist?

No. The blocking only needs grey boxes, simple mannequins and a camera. An AI agent connected to the Unreal editor can build that from a plain description, and you adjust the timing yourself.

Will the AI copy the grey boxes and colours?

Not if you tell it not to. State in the prompt that the video defines only camera, timing and layout, and that no materials, colours or lighting come from it. Flat, unlit proxies make this easier.

What resolution and frame rate should the reference be?

1920x1080 at 24 frames per second is a good default. Keep it frame-aligned and the same length as the clip you want back.

How do I record a live performance as the reference?

Record from the running engine while the performance happens, for example with Runtime Video Recorder, which captures hardware-encoded MP4 on desktop, mobile and VR and can lock to an exact frame rate.
